BMR Formulas Compared: Mifflin-St Jeor, Harris-Benedict and Katch-McArdle

📅 October 26, 2026 ⏱️ 9 min read ✍️ ToolPond

The same person, three formulas, three answers, and how to choose between them

For most people, Mifflin-St Jeor is the best default BMR formula. Use Katch-McArdle only if you know your body fat percentage reasonably well, and treat Harris-Benedict as a useful cross-check that runs slightly higher. The three formulas usually land within about 100 to 200 calories of each other, which is small compared with the error in guessing your activity level.

Quick answer

For an 80 kg, 180 cm, 30-year-old man with 20% body fat: Mifflin-St Jeor gives 1,780 calories, revised Harris-Benedict gives 1,854, and Katch-McArdle gives 1,752. Choose a formula in the calorie calculator and compare them yourself.

What BMR is and how it differs from RMR and TDEE

  • BMR (basal metabolic rate): the energy your body uses at complete rest to keep you alive: breathing, circulation, temperature and cell repair.
  • RMR (resting metabolic rate): very close to BMR and measured under easier conditions. The formulas on this page are technically estimates of resting energy, but people call them BMR.
  • TDEE (total daily energy expenditure): BMR plus the calories from movement, exercise and digesting food. This is the number you eat around to maintain, lose or gain weight.

Typically BMR is about 60 to 70 percent of a sedentary person's TDEE, so a small error in BMR carries through to your daily target.

The three formulas

Use weight (W) in kilograms, height (H) in centimeters and age (A) in years. To convert, divide pounds by 2.2046 and multiply inches by 2.54.

For Katch-McArdle, lean body mass = weight × (1 − body fat percentage). A person weighing 80 kg at 20% body fat has 64 kg of lean mass. The original 1919 Harris-Benedict equation also exists, but the 1984 revision is the version most tools use.

Worked calculation: the 80 kg man

  • Mifflin-St Jeor: 10 × 80 + 6.25 × 180 − 5 × 30 + 5 = 800 + 1,125 − 150 + 5 = 1,780
  • Harris-Benedict: 88.362 + 13.397 × 80 + 4.799 × 180 − 5.677 × 30 = 88.362 + 1,071.76 + 863.82 − 170.31 = 1,854
  • Katch-McArdle: 370 + 21.6 × 64 = 370 + 1,382.4 = 1,752

Which formula is most accurate

A widely cited 2005 review by the Academy of Nutrition and Dietetics compared several equations against measured resting metabolic rate and found Mifflin-St Jeor was most often within 10 percent of the measured value for both non-obese and obese adults. That is why it is the usual default in modern calculators.

No formula is exact. Two people with the same height, weight, age and sex can differ by several hundred calories in real life because of muscle mass, genetics, thyroid function, medications, sleep and other factors. These equations give an average for people like you, not a measurement of you.

You can estimate body fat with the body fat calculator, which is useful for Katch-McArdle, though estimates from a tape measure are rougher than a scan.

How to check your number in real life

  1. Calculate maintenance calories with one formula and an honest activity level.
  2. Eat about that amount for 2 to 3 weeks and weigh yourself under the same conditions, ideally each morning.
  3. If your weekly average weight is stable, your estimate is close.
  4. If it drifts up or down, shift calories by 100 to 200 and try again. Your own results beat any formula.

Frequently Asked Questions

Which BMR formula is the most accurate?
Mifflin-St Jeor is generally considered the most accurate for the general population, and a 2005 dietetics review found it within 10 percent of measured resting metabolism more often than the other common equations. Katch-McArdle can be better for very lean or muscular people who know their body fat.
What is the difference between Mifflin-St Jeor and Harris-Benedict?
Mifflin-St Jeor (1990) is the newer equation and tends to give a lower result. The revised Harris-Benedict equation (1984) usually runs 50 to 150 calories higher. Both use weight, height, age and sex.
Is Katch-McArdle better than Mifflin-St Jeor?
Only if your body fat percentage is accurate. It uses lean body mass, so it fits muscular or very lean people. A rough body fat guess can make it less reliable than Mifflin-St Jeor.
Why do calculators give different BMR numbers?
Each uses a different equation built from different research groups. The results usually differ by 100 to 200 calories, which is small compared with the uncertainty in your activity level.
What is the BMR formula for women?
Mifflin-St Jeor for women: 10 × weight (kg) + 6.25 × height (cm) − 5 × age − 161. A 65 kg, 165 cm, 35-year-old woman gets about 1,345 calories.
Is BMR the same as the calories I should eat?
No. BMR is what you burn at rest. Multiply it by an activity factor to get TDEE, then subtract calories to lose weight or add calories to gain.
Can BMR formulas be wrong for me?
Yes. They estimate averages. Muscle mass, genetics, medical conditions and medications can change your real number, so track your weight for a few weeks and adjust.

Conclusion

Start with Mifflin-St Jeor, cross-check with Harris-Benedict, and switch to Katch-McArdle only if you have a trustworthy body fat figure. The gap between them is modest, so spend your effort on an honest activity level and on tracking your weight trend. Try all three in the calorie calculator.

This guide gives estimates from standard equations and is not medical or dietary advice. Consult a doctor or registered dietitian for personal nutrition needs.
