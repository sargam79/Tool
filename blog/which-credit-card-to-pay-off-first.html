Which Credit Card Should You Pay Off First?

📅 October 27, 2026 ⏱️ 9 min read ✍️ ToolPond

Avalanche vs snowball, with a three-card example showing months to payoff and total interest

Pay the minimum on every card, then put all your extra money on the card with the highest interest rate (APR). This is called the avalanche method and it costs the least in interest. If you need quick wins to stay motivated, the snowball method, which pays the smallest balance first, costs a little more but works well for many people.

Quick answer

In the three-card example below ($6,300 total, $450 a month), avalanche clears the debt in 17 months with about $1,112 in interest, snowball takes 17 months with about $1,322, and paying only the minimums takes about 10 years and roughly $8,846. The two methods

  • Avalanche: list your cards by APR, highest first. Pay the minimum on all cards and send every extra dollar to the highest-APR card. When it is gone, roll its whole payment onto the next one.
  • Snowball: list your cards by balance, smallest first. Pay the minimum on all cards and send every extra dollar to the smallest balance. When it is gone, roll that payment onto the next smallest.

In both methods you keep paying the minimum on every card. Missing a minimum payment can trigger late fees and, with some issuers, a higher penalty APR, which undoes the benefit of any strategy.

A three-card example

Imagine three cards with a combined $6,300 in debt. You can afford $450 a month in total, and no new purchases are added.

Card A$1,50019.99%$40
Card B$4,00027.99%$100
Card C$80015.99%$25

The minimums total $165, so $285 a month is extra. Interest is charged monthly at APR ÷ 12 on the remaining balance. The simplified minimums above stay fixed, while real minimums usually shrink with the balance.

  • Avalanche order: Card B (27.99%), then Card A (19.99%), then Card C (15.99%).
  • Snowball order: Card C ($800), then Card A ($1,500), then Card B ($4,000).

Two things stand out. First, the order matters far less than paying more than the minimum: both methods save over $7,500 compared with minimums only. Second, avalanche saves about $210 in this example because it attacks the 27.99% card first, while snowball gives you your first win in month 3 instead of month 13.

Which one should you choose?

If you...Choose
Want the lowest total interest and are comfortable waiting for a winAvalanche
Have one card with a much higher APR than the restAvalanche
Have tried and quit debt plans before, or need early progressSnowball
Have several tiny balances you could clear quicklySnowball
Have similar APRs on all cardsSnowball (little cost to the difference)

The best method is the one you will actually keep doing. The $210 difference above is real, but dropping out of a plan costs far more.

Cases where the order changes

  • A 0% promotional rate that is about to end: if a card will jump to a high APR on a known date, pay it down enough before then, even if it is not the highest rate now.
  • A card near its credit limit: high utilization can lower your credit score. Many lenders prefer to see usage well below your limit, so paying down a nearly maxed card can help your score as well as save interest.
  • Equal APRs: if two cards share a rate, pay the smaller balance first.
  • An annual fee card you plan to keep: the fee does not change the order, but factor it into whether the card is worth keeping at all.
  • A balance transfer: moving a high-APR balance to a lower or 0% offer can cut interest, but check the transfer fee (often 3% to 5%) and the date the promotion ends.

Step by step

  1. List every card with its balance, APR and minimum payment.
  2. Add up your minimums and decide how much you can pay in total each month.
  3. Pick avalanche or snowball and rank your cards.
  4. Set automatic minimum payments on every card so you never miss one.
  5. Send the extra amount to your target card each month.
  6. When a card is paid off, add its full payment to the next card in the order.
  7. Stop adding new charges to the cards you are paying off, or the plan will not shrink.

If you also have loans, the principles are similar. See how to pay off a loan faster, and plan your monthly amount with the budget planner.

Mistakes to avoid

  • Skipping minimums on other cards to pour money onto one card.
  • Closing paid-off cards right away. Closing a card can reduce your available credit and shorten your account history, which may affect your score. Keep it open if it has no fee.
  • Using the card again while you are paying it off.
  • Ignoring the APR on cash advances, which is often higher than the purchase rate and has no grace period.
  • Not updating the plan when rates, balances or income change.

Frequently Asked Questions

Which credit card should I pay off first?
Pay the minimum on all cards, then put extra money on the card with the highest APR (avalanche), because it costs the least interest. If you need quick motivation, pay the smallest balance first (snowball).
Is it better to pay off the highest interest card or the smallest balance?
Highest interest saves more money. In the three-card example in this guide, avalanche saved about $210 over snowball. Smallest balance first gives earlier wins, which helps some people stick with the plan.
Should I pay off the card with the highest balance first?
Not necessarily. Balance size matters only for the snowball method, and then it is the smallest balance that goes first. Under avalanche, the APR decides the order.
Does paying off a credit card hurt my credit score?
Paying down balances usually helps, because lower utilization is generally viewed positively. Closing the card after paying it off can lower your available credit, so many people keep no-fee cards open.
How long will it take to pay off my credit cards?
It depends on your balances, APRs and monthly payment. In the example here, $6,300 across three cards took 17 months at $450 a month, but about 10 years when paying only minimums.
Should I use a balance transfer?
It can help if the new rate is much lower and the transfer fee, often 3% to 5%, is smaller than the interest you would save. Check when the promotional rate ends and avoid new charges on the old card.
Can I pay more than the minimum every month?
Yes. Most issuers allow extra payments with no penalty, and any extra amount goes toward the balance and reduces future interest.

Conclusion

Pay every minimum, then send all extra money to the highest-APR card for the lowest cost, or to the smallest balance if you need early wins. Either beats minimum payments by a wide margin. Plug in your own balances and rates in the credit card payoff calculator to see your payoff date.

This guide is for general information and is not financial advice. The example uses simplified fixed minimum payments and no new charges. Check your card agreements for exact terms, and consider a nonprofit credit counselor if you are struggling with debt.
