How to Calculate Take-Home Pay From Your Gross Salary (2026)

📅 October 11, 2026 ⏱️ 9 min read ✍️ ToolPond

Gross vs net pay explained step by step, with worked examples you can check in the calculator

Your salary is not what lands in your bank account. Take-home pay (net pay) is what is left after income tax, Social Security, Medicare, state tax and any deductions taken from your paycheck. This guide shows how each piece is worked out, using 2026 US federal figures, so you can estimate your own number with our salary calculator.

Quick answer

Take-home pay = gross pay − federal income tax − Social Security (6.2%) − Medicare (1.45%) − state tax − pre-tax deductions. A single filer earning $60,000 with no state income tax pays about $9,610 in tax and takes home about $50,390 a year, or $4,199 a month.

Gross pay vs net pay

Gross pay is your salary before anything is taken out. Net pay, or take-home pay, is what you actually receive. The difference is made up of taxes and any deductions, such as retirement contributions, that your employer withholds.

Step 1: Federal income tax

US federal income tax is progressive: only the income inside each bracket is taxed at that bracket's rate. First you subtract the standard deduction, which for 2026 is $16,100 for a single filer, $32,200 for married filing jointly and $24,150 for head of household. The rest is your taxable income.

A common mistake is thinking a raise into a higher bracket reduces your whole income. It does not. Only the dollars above the bracket line are taxed at the higher rate.

Step 2: Social Security and Medicare (FICA)

  • Social Security: 6.2% of your wages, up to a yearly wage limit. For 2026 that limit is $184,500, so the most an employee pays is about $11,439.
  • Medicare: 1.45% of all wages. Higher earners may owe an extra Medicare tax above certain income levels.

Together that is 7.65% of pay for most employees. These taxes apply to your gross wages, and they are not reduced by the standard deduction.

Step 3: State income tax

States differ a lot. Texas, Florida and Washington have no state income tax on wages. Others use a flat rate or their own brackets. Our calculator uses a single simplified rate for each state it lists, which is useful for a rough comparison but will not match your exact state return. Local taxes, such as city income tax, are not included.

A full worked example

Take a single filer with a $60,000 salary and no extra deductions.

1

Taxable income

$60,000 − $16,100 standard deduction = $43,900.

2

Federal tax

10% × $12,400 = $1,240, plus 12% × ($43,900 − $12,400) = $3,780. Total $5,020.

3

Social Security and Medicare

$60,000 × 6.2% = $3,720, and $60,000 × 1.45% = $870.

4

Take-home pay

$60,000 − $5,020 − $3,720 − $870 = $50,390 a year, with no state tax.

Your marginal federal rate here is 12%, because the last dollars you earned fall in the 12% bracket. Your effective federal rate is lower: $5,020 ÷ $60,000 = about 8.4%. Counting every tax, the total is 16% in Texas.

About the California figure

A single flat 9.3% is a simplification. Actual California tax is progressive and, for a $60,000 salary, is usually lower than this estimate, so treat that row as a cautious figure, not a tax return.

Example with a family

A married couple filing jointly with a combined $90,000 salary and two children, in a state with no income tax:

  • Taxable income: $90,000 − $32,200 = $57,800.
  • Tax before credits: 10% × $24,800 = $2,480, plus 12% × $33,000 = $3,960, which is $6,440.
  • Child tax credit: 2 × $2,200 = $4,400, leaving federal tax of $2,040.
  • Social Security $5,580 and Medicare $1,305.
  • Take-home: $90,000 − $2,040 − $5,580 − $1,305 = $81,075 a year, or about $6,756 a month.

The calculator applies the full credit per dependent and does not model income phase-outs, so it fits typical middle incomes best.

How a 401(k) changes take-home pay

Pre-tax retirement contributions lower your taxable income, so they lower your income tax, but they do not lower Social Security and Medicare, which still apply to the full wage. A single filer earning $75,000 in a state with a 6.5% simplified rate (the calculator's New York setting) compares like this:

No contribution$6,000 contributed

Putting $6,000 into the plan reduces take-home pay by $4,290, not $6,000, because about $1,710 comes back as lower tax. The $6,000 goes into your own account, so you still own it.

Converting to monthly and per-paycheck pay

Pay period conversions
Monthly = annual ÷ 12
Every two weeks = annual ÷ 26
Twice a month = annual ÷ 24
Weekly = annual ÷ 52

$50,390 ÷ 12 = $4,199 a month
$50,390 ÷ 26 = $1,938 every two weeks

What this estimate leaves out

Not included
  • Health insurance premiums and other pre-tax benefits (HSA, FSA, commuter).
  • Local and city taxes, and state disability or family-leave contributions.
  • Additional Medicare tax for high earners, and tax credits other than the child tax credit.
  • Bonuses and overtime, which may be withheld at a different rate than regular pay.
  • Your real withholding (Form W-4), which can differ from the tax you actually owe at year end.

Treat the result as a planning estimate. Your pay stub shows the real amounts.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much of my salary goes to taxes?
For a single filer earning $60,000 with no state income tax, about 16% goes to federal income tax, Social Security and Medicare combined. With a state income tax it is higher. The share rises as income rises because of progressive brackets.
What is the difference between marginal and effective tax rate?
The marginal rate is the rate on your last dollar of income, such as 12%. The effective rate is total tax divided by total income, which is lower because the first dollars are taxed at lower rates or covered by the standard deduction.
Does a 401(k) contribution reduce my take-home pay?
Yes, but by less than the amount you contribute, because traditional 401(k) contributions lower your taxable income. Social Security and Medicare are still calculated on the full wage.
Why does my paycheck differ from the calculator?
Real paychecks include health premiums, local taxes, benefit deductions and your W-4 withholding choices. The calculator uses a simplified method and a single estimated rate per state, so use it for planning rather than as an exact figure.
How do I calculate hourly pay to yearly pay?
Multiply the hourly rate by hours per week, then by 52. For example, $25 an hour at 40 hours a week is $25 × 40 × 52 = $52,000 a year before tax.

Conclusion

Take-home pay comes down to four pieces: federal income tax with its standard deduction and brackets, Social Security and Medicare at 7.65%, your state's tax, and any deductions from your paycheck. Work through them once by hand to see how they fit together, then use the salary calculator to compare scenarios. If you are planning a budget around the result, try our budget planner.
