Understanding Calorie Needs: Your Personal Nutrition Guide

Knowing your daily calorie needs is fundamental to achieving any fitness goal. Whether you want to lose weight, build muscle, or maintain your current physique, understanding how many calories your body needs each day is the first step toward success.

What is TDEE? TDEE stands for Total Daily Energy Expenditure—the total number of calories your body burns in a day. It includes your Basal Metabolic Rate (BMR, calories burned at rest) plus calories burned through daily activities and exercise. Your TDEE is unique to your age, sex, height, weight, and activity level.

Why calculate your calorie needs? Knowing your TDEE allows you to:

  • Lose weight strategically: Eat 250-500 calories below TDEE for steady weight loss of roughly 0.5-1 lb (0.25-0.5 kg) a week
  • Build muscle: Eat 250-500 calories above TDEE with proper strength training
  • Maintain weight: Eat at your TDEE to keep your current body composition
  • Track progress: Monitor calorie intake against your calculated needs
  • Optimize nutrition: Plan macronutrients (protein, carbs, fat) based on your calorie target

The science behind it: Your body burns calories constantly—even at rest. Your BMR accounts for 60-75% of daily calorie burn. Activity level multiplies your BMR to get TDEE. Someone sedentary might have a TDEE of 2,000 calories, while an active person of the same size might burn 2,800 calories daily. Understanding this helps you set realistic nutrition goals.

Which one should you pick?

  • Mifflin-St Jeor is the default because it is widely used and has been validated in studies of healthy adults, including people with overweight.
  • Revised Harris-Benedict is an older equation and often gives a slightly higher result. It is useful as a second opinion.
  • Katch-McArdle ignores sex and age and uses lean mass instead, so it can suit muscular or very lean people better, if you know your body fat percentage. If you only guess it, the result is only as good as the guess. Use our body fat calculator for an estimate.

No equation can see your genetics, hormones or muscle distribution, so treat any result as a starting point and adjust it using your real weight trend over two to four weeks.

Calorie Deficit Calculator: How Big Should It Be?

A calorie deficit is the gap between what you eat and what you burn. The usual rule of thumb is that about 3,500 kcal equals 1 lb of body weight (about 7,700 kcal per kg), so the size of your daily deficit sets how fast the scale moves.

Frequently Asked Questions

How many calories should I eat a day to lose weight? ▼

Start with your TDEE (maintenance calories), then subtract 250 to 500 kcal for steady loss of roughly 0.5 to 1 lb (0.25 to 0.5 kg) a week. If your TDEE is 2,200 kcal, that means about 1,700 to 1,950 kcal a day. Many guidelines treat 1,200 kcal (women) and 1,500 kcal (men) as the lowest level to use without medical supervision.

How many calories do I need to cut to lose 1 pound a week? ▼

About 500 kcal a day. One pound of body weight is commonly estimated at 3,500 kcal, so 500 kcal × 7 days = 3,500 kcal. In metric terms, 1 kg is about 7,700 kcal, so losing 1 kg a week needs roughly 1,100 kcal a day, which is too aggressive for many people.

What is a calorie deficit? ▼

A calorie deficit means eating fewer calories than you burn. If your TDEE is 2,300 kcal and you eat 1,800 kcal, your deficit is 500 kcal a day. A moderate deficit of 10 to 25% of maintenance is easier to sustain and protects muscle better than a crash diet.

What is the difference between BMR and TDEE? ▼

BMR (basal metabolic rate) is the energy you burn at rest. TDEE (total daily energy expenditure) is BMR multiplied by an activity factor, so it includes walking, work and exercise. TDEE is the number that matters for weight management. For example, a BMR of 1,500 kcal can mean a TDEE of about 2,300 kcal at moderate activity.

How do I calculate calories to reach a goal weight by a date? ▼

Subtract your goal weight from your current weight, multiply by 3,500 (per pound) and divide by the number of days. Losing 10 lb in 10 weeks needs 35,000 ÷ 70 = 500 kcal a day below maintenance. Enter a goal weight and the number of weeks in the calculator above and it does the sum, with a warning if the pace is too fast.

Is 1,200 calories a day enough? ▼

For most adults, 1,200 kcal is very low and hard to keep up. It is often cited as a minimum for women and 1,500 kcal for men, and only under medical or dietitian supervision. A smaller deficit over a longer time is safer and easier to maintain.

How many calories should I eat to build muscle? ▼

Aim for a small surplus of about 250 to 500 kcal above maintenance, together with strength training and enough protein (roughly 1.6 to 2.2 g per kg of body weight). A larger surplus mostly adds body fat rather than extra muscle.

Which formula is best: Mifflin-St Jeor, Harris-Benedict or Katch-McArdle? ▼

Mifflin-St Jeor is the usual default because it has been validated in studies of healthy adults. Revised Harris-Benedict is an older equation that often gives a slightly higher result. Katch-McArdle uses lean body mass, so it can suit very lean or muscular people if you know your body fat percentage. The calculator shows all three so you can compare.

How accurate is this calorie calculator? ▼

It gives an estimate, not a measurement. In validation studies the Mifflin-St Jeor equation predicted resting energy use within 10% for most adults, but not for everyone. Use the result as a starting point, track your weight for 2 to 4 weeks, and adjust by 100 to 200 kcal if the trend does not match your goal.

Why am I not losing weight at my calculated calories? ▼

Common reasons are underestimating how much you eat (portion sizes and cooking oils add up), overestimating activity, water-weight swings that hide fat loss for a week or two, and the fact that your needs fall as you lose weight. Track carefully for two weeks, then reduce intake by 100 to 200 kcal if your average weight has not moved.

How often should I recalculate my calories? ▼

Recalculate every 10 to 15 lb (4.5 to 7 kg) of weight change, or when your activity changes a lot. Someone who weighs 150 lb burns fewer calories than they did at 180 lb, so old targets drift out of date.

Should macros be 30/40/30 (protein/carbs/fat)? ▼

It is a reasonable starting point, not a rule. Set protein first (about 1.6 to 2.2 g per kg if you lift weights), keep fat at about 20 to 35% of calories, and fill the rest with carbohydrates based on how you feel and train. The calculator offers a higher-protein and a lower-carb split too.

Can I eat different amounts on rest days and training days? ▼

Yes. Some people eat a little more on training days and a little less on rest days, as long as the weekly average matches their target. Keeping calories steady is also fine, so choose whichever is easier to stick to.

Is my data private? ▼

Yes. All calculations happen in your browser. We never see, store or collect your height, weight, age or other personal data, and no login is required. The site itself may show ads and use analytics, as described in our Privacy Policy.

Real-World Examples

Example 1: Sedentary office worker who wants to lose weight

Profile: 30-year-old man, 180 lb (81.6 kg), 5'10" (177.8 cm), desk job with little exercise (factor 1.2).

Results (Mifflin-St Jeor): BMR 1,783 kcal, TDEE 2,139 kcal. A 500 kcal deficit gives 1,639 kcal a day, about 1 lb (0.45 kg) of loss a week. On a 30/40/30 split that is 123 g protein, 164 g carbs and 55 g fat.

Example 2: Very active woman who wants to gain muscle

Profile: 25-year-old woman, 140 lb (63.5 kg), 5'6" (167.6 cm), hard training 6 days a week (factor 1.725).

Results (Mifflin-St Jeor): BMR 1,397 kcal, TDEE 2,409 kcal. A lean-gain surplus of 250 kcal gives 2,659 kcal a day. On a 30/40/30 split that is 199 g protein, 266 g carbs and 89 g fat.

Example 3: Losing 20 lb in 20 weeks

Profile: 35-year-old woman, 170 lb, 5'5", moderately active (factor 1.55), goal weight 150 lb.

Plan: BMR 1,467 kcal, TDEE 2,274 kcal. Losing 20 lb takes about 70,000 kcal (20 × 3,500), which over 140 days is a 500 kcal daily deficit, so the target is 1,774 kcal a day. That is 1 lb a week, about 0.6% of body weight, inside the commonly suggested limit of 1% a week. Because your needs fall as you lose weight, recalculate every 10 lb or so.

Method and limits

Estimates resting energy use with the Mifflin-St Jeor, revised Harris-Benedict or Katch-McArdle equation and multiplies it by an activity factor. Goal calories use about 3,500 kcal per pound (7,700 kcal per kg) of body weight change, which is a rule of thumb, and assume your needs stay constant. Real needs vary from person to person, so treat the result as a starting point and adjust it based on how your body responds. Speak to a doctor or dietitian before a major diet change.
